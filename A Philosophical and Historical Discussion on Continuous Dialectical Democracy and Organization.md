**Democracy** is a system of governance in which the source of political power rests with the people, leaders are chosen through free and fair elections, and fundamental human rights are constitutionally guaranteed.

In political science literature (for example, in the works of thinkers like Robert Dahl, Joseph Schumpeter, and Amartya Sen), democracy is defined not merely as a ballot-box mechanism, but as an institutional system integrated with the rule of law, separation of powers, freedom of expression, and pluralism.

---

Core Elements of Democracy from a Scientific Perspective

Political scientists look for the presence of the following fundamental criteria for a system to be considered democratic:

* **Political Participation and Elections:** The right of citizens to elect and replace their leaders through competitive elections held at regular intervals, based on secret ballots and open counting.  
* **Rule of Law:** Equality of everyone, including rulers, before the constitution and laws, along with judicial independence.  
* **Fundamental Rights and Freedoms:** Protection of freedom of thought, expression, press, assembly, and association.  
* **Pluralism and Minority Rights:** The ability of different views and identities to express themselves, and the principle that the majority must not violate the fundamental rights of the minority when making decisions.

---

The prominent strengths and structural criticisms regarding the functioning of democracy in academic studies are summarized below:

Mechanisms by Which a Multi-Party Appearance Protects "Fake Democracy"

Critical political sociologists (such as Roberto Michels, Chantal Mouffe, and Richard Katz) explain how increasing the number of parties can mask genuine democracy through three core dynamics:

* **Illusion of Choice (Fake Competition):** If all parties with different names and emblems defend the same economic order, the same capital relations, and the same elite mode of governance, the multiplicity presented to voters is not a genuine alternative, but merely a **rotation of ruling cadres**.  
* **Fragmentation of Social Opposition:** When the common economic and democratic demands of the people are divided into dozens of small parties formed over artificial identity or leadership splits—rather than uniting in a single strong grassroots organization—social energy is exhausted and the existing status quo is preserved.  
* **Diffusion of Political Responsibility ("Cartel of Parties"):** Within multi-party coalitions or look-alike government-opposition balances, rather than genuinely holding one another accountable, parties transform into a joint structure sharing state resources and privileges; the people, despite going to the ballot box, cannot change the system.
